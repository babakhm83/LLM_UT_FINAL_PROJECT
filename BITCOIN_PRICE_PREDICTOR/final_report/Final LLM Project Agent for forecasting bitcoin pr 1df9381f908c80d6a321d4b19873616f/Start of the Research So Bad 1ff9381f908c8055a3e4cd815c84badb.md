# **The EU Net-Zero Industry Act: Forging a Path to Climate Neutrality and Industrial Competitiveness**

Executive Summary

The European Union's Net-Zero Industry Act (NZIA) stands as a cornerstone of the Green Deal Industrial Plan, representing a strategic initiative to accelerate the EU's transition to climate neutrality while simultaneously bolstering its industrial strength and resilience. Conceived in response to escalating global competition in clean technologies and the urgent need to secure supply chains, the NZIA aims to significantly scale up the manufacturing capacity of net-zero technologies within the Union. It sets ambitious benchmarks, including meeting at least 40% of the EU's annual deployment needs for strategic net-zero technologies by 2030 and establishing substantial CO2 storage capacity.

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the NZIA, examining its legislative framework, core provisions, and the ambitious objectives it seeks to achieve. It delves into the critical May 2025 secondary legislation, which provides further clarity on the Act's scope, the application of non-price criteria in public auctions, and the selection process for strategic projects. A significant focus is placed on the funding mechanisms underpinning the NZIA, scrutinizing the reliance on existing EU funds such as the Innovation Fund, InvestEU, and REPowerEU, and addressing the persistent debate around the need for "new money" and the evolution of concepts like the European Sovereignty Fund.

The economic and trade implications of the NZIA are explored in depth, including its potential impacts on job creation, investment, and GDP growth, alongside the complex challenge of enhancing EU competitiveness and supply chain resilience, particularly in relation to dominant global players like China. The report analyzes the Act's provisions through the lens of WTO compatibility, assessing the delicate balance between fostering domestic industry and adhering to international trade rules.

Diverse stakeholder reactions are presented, capturing the perspectives of key industry associations, which largely support the NZIA's goals but call for more assertive measures and clearer frameworks, and non-governmental organizations, which raise concerns about environmental safeguards, public participation, and the Act's technological scope. Furthermore, the report examines the multifaceted challenges of national implementation, highlighting general hurdles and providing insights into the specific strategies and difficulties encountered by selected Member States.

The NZIA's potential to reshape EU industry is substantial, offering a pathway to technological leadership and reduced strategic dependencies. However, its success hinges on overcoming significant obstacles, including securing adequate and sustained funding, ensuring consistent and effective implementation across all Member States, navigating the inherent trade-offs between speed and due diligence, and fostering a supportive ecosystem for innovation and skills development. The May 2025 implementing measures mark a crucial step in operationalizing the Act, but the journey towards achieving its long-term vision will require continuous political commitment, adaptive governance, and robust collaboration between public and private actors. The NZIA is not a singular solution but a critical component of a broader, evolving strategy to position the EU at the forefront of the global net-zero industrial age.

**I. The EU Net-Zero Industry Act: Framework and Ambitions**

**A. Genesis and Objectives: Context within the Green Deal Industrial Plan and response to global pressures (e.g., US IRA, China's dominance).**

The Net-Zero Industry Act (NZIA) emerges as a critical legislative initiative within the broader strategy of the European Green Deal Industrial Plan, designed to significantly ramp up the manufacturing of clean technologies across the European Union.1 Its conception is intrinsically linked to the EU's ambition to achieve climate neutrality by 2050 and to navigate an increasingly competitive global landscape for green technologies. The Act is, in part, a strategic response to proactive industrial policies enacted by other major economies, notably the United States' Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) and China's long-standing dominance in several net-zero technology sectors.2

The European Union currently finds itself in a position of net importer for many crucial net-zero technologies. This dependency is particularly acute in specific segments; for instance, the EU relies on China for over 90% of its solar photovoltaic wafers and ingots, and a significant portion of its batteries.5 Such dependencies have been brought into sharp focus by recent geopolitical events, including the energy crisis exacerbated by the war in Ukraine, underscoring the need for greater European strategic autonomy in these vital areas.3 The NZIA, therefore, is not merely an environmental or industrial policy but carries substantial geopolitical weight. It reflects a concerted effort by the EU to reclaim a degree of technological sovereignty and mitigate the risks associated with over-reliance on external suppliers for technologies fundamental to its green transition and energy security.

The global market for key mass-manufactured net-zero technologies is experiencing exponential growth, with projections indicating it could triple in value by 2030 to an estimated €600 billion annually.1 The NZIA aims to position the EU to capture a significant share of this expanding market, thereby enhancing the competitiveness of its industries, fostering innovation, and ensuring secure access to the technologies necessary for its decarbonization pathway.7

However, the timing of the NZIA, proposed in March 2023 2 following the US IRA's passage in August 2022 2, suggests a degree of reactivity in the EU's strategy. This places the Union in a challenging position, endeavoring to catch up in sectors where other nations have already established considerable manufacturing capacities and economies of scale. Reports from early 2025 highlighted "worrying signals of manufacturing capacity moving abroad" and an "inability to scale up industrial production in Europe," further emphasizing the urgency and difficulty of this undertaking.10 The Act's core objectives thus revolve around creating a more favorable regulatory environment, stimulating investment, and accelerating the deployment of net-zero manufacturing capabilities across the Union to meet both its climate targets and its aspirations for enhanced industrial leadership and resilience.7

**B. Core Provisions and Scope: Key legislative elements, targeted technologies (including the list of 19 net-zero technologies), and manufacturing benchmarks.**

The Net-Zero Industry Act establishes a comprehensive framework designed to bolster the EU's manufacturing capacity in clean technologies. Central to this framework are ambitious manufacturing benchmarks: the Act aims for the Union's overall strategic net-zero technologies manufacturing capacity to approach or reach at least 40% of its annual deployment needs by 2030.1 Looking further ahead, a 2040 benchmark targets capturing 15% of the global production value in these technologies.10 Alongside these manufacturing goals, the NZIA sets a specific target for carbon capture and storage (CCS), mandating an annual CO2 injection capacity of at least 50 million tonnes in geological storage sites within the EU by 2030.8 Licensed oil and gas producers are obligated to contribute to this target on a pro rata basis, reflecting their historical production levels.3

The scope of the NZIA is broad, officially encompassing a list of 19 net-zero technologies deemed critical for the EU's decarbonization efforts.10 These include:

1. Solar technologies (photovoltaic, solar thermal electric, and solar thermal).
2. Onshore wind and offshore renewable technologies.
3. Battery and energy storage technologies.
4. Heat pumps and geothermal energy technologies.
5. Hydrogen technologies, including electrolysers and fuel cells.
6. Sustainable biogas/biomethane technologies.
7. Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) technologies.
8. Grid technologies.
9. Nuclear fission energy technologies, including nuclear fuel cycle technologies, advanced technologies to produce energy from nuclear processes with minimal waste from the fuel cycle, small modular reactors (SMRs), and related best-in-class fuels.
10. Sustainable alternative fuels technologies (non-biological origin).
11. Hydropower technologies.
12. Renewable energy technologies not otherwise listed.
13. Energy efficiency technologies for energy systems.
14. Transformative industrial technologies for decarbonising energy-intensive industries.
15. Technologies for producing sustainable materials from biogenic or synthetic routes that substitute fossil-intensive materials.
16. Recycling technologies for critical raw materials.
17. Advanced manufacturing technologies for the production of net-zero technologies.
18. Maritime net-zero technologies (e.g., wind-assisted propulsion).
19. Aviation net-zero technologies (e.g., efficient aircraft design, sustainable aviation fuels).

The May 2025 secondary legislation further refined the specific components falling under these categories.16

To achieve its objectives, the NZIA introduces several key provisions:

- **Streamlined Permitting:** The Act mandates the establishment of "one-stop shops" at the national level to simplify and accelerate permitting procedures for net-zero manufacturing projects. It sets binding maximum timelines for permit decisions, generally ranging from 9 to 18 months depending on project scale and strategic importance.3
- **Net-Zero Strategic Projects:** Certain projects can be designated as "Net-Zero Strategic Projects," granting them priority status at the national level. This includes even faster permitting, focused attention from the Net-Zero Europe Platform (including financing advice), and urgent treatment in judicial and dispute resolution procedures.8
- **Market Access and Demand Support:** The Act aims to boost demand for EU-made net-zero technologies through the application of non-price criteria (such as sustainability, resilience, and cybersecurity) in public procurement procedures and renewable energy auctions.8
- **Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys:** Member States are empowered to designate specific geographical areas as "Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys." These Valleys are intended to become clusters of net-zero industrial activity, benefiting from further streamlined administrative processes and potentially pre-assessed environmental impacts for certain sectors.11
- **Skills Development:** The NZIA supports the establishment of "Net-Zero Industry Academies" to develop the necessary skilled workforce for the expanding clean tech sector.15
- **Innovation Support:** The Act includes provisions for "regulatory sandboxes" to allow for the testing of innovative net-zero technologies under flexible regulatory conditions, fostering experimentation and faster market entry for novel solutions.2

The inclusion of a wide array of 19 technologies, alongside support for others like advanced nuclear and sustainable aviation fuels, reflects a complex political balancing act. While this approach caters to diverse Member State interests and acknowledges multiple technological pathways to decarbonization, it has drawn criticism for potentially diluting strategic focus and resources.10 The "strategic project" designation is an attempt to introduce prioritization within this broad scope, but the sheer number of eligible technologies could still present challenges in resource allocation and effective support.

Similarly, the 40% manufacturing benchmark serves as a potent political signal and a long-term directional guide for investors and industry.8 However, its non-binding nature means that its ultimate achievement will heavily depend on the consistent commitment of Member States and the effective implementation of the Act's supporting measures, particularly the market-pull mechanisms like reformed public procurement rules. The EU's past experiences with non-binding targets have yielded mixed results, underscoring the importance of robust enforcement and complementary policies for the NZIA's benchmarks to translate into tangible industrial growth.

**C. Official Timeline: From proposal to entry into force and key milestones.**

The legislative journey of the Net-Zero Industry Act has been characterized by a sense of urgency, reflecting the EU's determination to respond swiftly to global challenges and accelerate its green transition. The European Commission tabled the initial proposal on March 16, 2023.2 A political agreement between the European Parliament and the Council was reached on February 6, 2024 12, paving the way for the formal adoption of Regulation (EU) 2024/1735 on June 13, 2024.22 The NZIA officially entered into force on June 29, 2024.10

Following the Act's entry into force, the Commission moved rapidly to develop the necessary secondary legislation. Public consultations on draft implementing and delegated acts were held in January and February 2025.19 This process culminated in the adoption of several key pieces of secondary legislation between May 23 and May 26, 2025, providing crucial details for the NZIA's operationalization.16

Several important milestones are set for the near future. The rules on non-price criteria in renewable energy auctions are slated to apply from December 30, 2025, or early January 2026 16, with Member States expected to begin implementing these new auction designs starting in 2026.19 The European Commission is also due to specify environmental sustainability requirements for public procurement through an implementing act in 2025.11 For the CO2 storage targets, obligated oil and gas producers were required to submit their contribution plans by June 2025, with the Commission anticipated to specify individual obligations in the first quarter of 2025.27 Looking further ahead, the Commission is mandated to assess the CO2 market by the end of June 2027 and review the overall storage target in 2028.27

This relatively rapid legislative pace, from initial proposal to the rollout of secondary legislation within approximately two years, signals a strong political will within the EU. However, such speed also presents challenges. It may strain the implementation capacity of Member States, which are tasked with transposing and enforcing these complex new rules. The period of 2025-2026 is particularly critical, as key provisions like non-price criteria and strategic project selection mechanisms become fully active. The initial experiences during this phase, including the outcomes of the first auctions conducted under the new rules 19, will be closely scrutinized and will significantly influence the NZIA's long-term effectiveness and credibility. Early successes could build momentum, while significant implementation hurdles could undermine the Act's ambitious objectives.

**II. Implementation and Governance: Navigating the Path Forward**

The successful translation of the Net-Zero Industry Act from legislative text into tangible industrial and environmental outcomes hinges on effective implementation and robust governance structures. The May 2025 secondary legislation package provides critical operational clarifications, while the Net-Zero Europe Platform is tasked with overseeing this complex process. Concurrently, the establishment of Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys aims to create localized hubs for rapid technological deployment and industrial clustering.

**A. The May 2025 Secondary Legislation: Detailed analysis of clarifications**

In late May 2025, the European Commission adopted a crucial package of secondary legislation, comprising three implementing acts and one delegated act, along with a communication, to further operationalize the Net-Zero Industry Act.16 These legal acts aim to provide greater clarity for businesses and public authorities on how to apply key NZIA provisions, particularly concerning the scope of covered technologies, the use of non-price criteria in public auctions, the definition and management of supply chain dependencies, and the selection criteria for strategic projects.16

1. Scope and strategic components (Delegated Regulation C(2025) 2901 final).

Delegated Regulation C(2025) 2901 final amends the Annex to the NZIA, significantly expanding and refining the list of specific components considered "always primarily used" in the production of net-zero technologies.17 This list is vital as it determines which elements can benefit from the NZIA's provisions. The update, which saw the addition of nearly 50 new components, was the result of an extensive consultation process involving Commission experts, Member State representatives, and specialized industrial sectors, including public feedback gathered in early 2025.17

The methodology for including components in this updated list is based on four key criteria: the specific nature of the component, its commercial availability, its priority use in net-zero technologies, and its essentiality for the functioning of these technologies.17 This structured approach aims to ensure that the NZIA targets genuinely critical elements of the clean tech supply chain. Importantly, the regulation allows for flexibility; project promoters can still seek recognition for components not explicitly listed if they can provide evidence to national competent authorities of their primary use in net-zero technologies.17 This dynamic approach to defining the scope allows the NZIA to adapt to technological evolution. However, this iterative process of updating lists, while ensuring accuracy and responsiveness to industry developments, can also introduce a degree of planning uncertainty for businesses that must continually monitor these classifications for eligibility.

2. Non-price criteria in renewable energy auctions (responsible business conduct, cybersecurity, sustainability, resilience).

Implementing Act C(2025)2900 details the pre-qualification and award criteria for renewable energy auctions.28 The Commission adopted specific rules for the inclusion of non-price criteria—such as responsible business conduct, cybersecurity, sustainability, and resilience contributions—in Member State auctions for deploying renewable energy.16 Commencing from December 30, 2025 (or January 2026), these non-price criteria must be applied to at least 30% of the auctioned volume annually per Member State, or to a minimum of 6 GW per year per Member State, whichever is higher.16

The legislation provides more detailed parameters to foster a harmonized application of these criteria across the EU, thereby offering greater predictability for businesses, while still permitting a degree of flexibility for Member States to tailor implementation to their specific contexts.16 The 30% threshold for applying non-price criteria represents a significant policy signal. However, its ultimate impact will depend on whether Member States view this as a minimum baseline to be surpassed or merely a target to be met. Industry groups, such as Eurofer, have advocated for a 100% application of non-price criteria to more decisively shift the market.30 The inherent flexibility, if not carefully managed and coordinated, could lead to the fragmented application feared by organizations like WindEurope, potentially undermining the creation of a level playing field within the EU single market.31

3. Defining and addressing "excessive dependency" for the resilience criterion.

A key element of the May 2025 package is the focus on supply chain resilience. Implementing Act C(2025)9033 lists net-zero technology final products and their main specific components that can trigger the mandatory application of the "resilience" non-price criterion in public procurement and auctions.16 This provision requires contracting authorities to consider supply chain resilience—not solely price—when awarding contracts, particularly if there is an "excessive dependency on a single source of supply" for a given technology or component.16

To support this, Communication C(2025)9034 provides data and information on the origins of the EU's supply of net-zero technologies, specifically highlighting dependencies on third countries.16 This information is intended to guide Member States in applying the resilience criterion and in evaluating projects for strategic status. The NZIA proposal itself had already pointed to significant EU dependencies, such as on China for over 90% of solar PV ingots and wafers.5

While the objective of reducing excessive dependencies is clear, the practical operationalization of "resilience" presents considerable challenges. The term "excessive dependency" is inherently qualitative, and the precise methodology for its assessment and consistent application across 27 Member States remains a complex issue, despite the Commission's communication.29 There is a risk that varying interpretations and applications could lead to de facto protectionism, potentially inviting challenges under WTO rules if the criteria are perceived as discriminatory or as disguised local content requirements.

4. Strategic project selection criteria and processes.

Implementing Decision C(2025)9035 offers guidelines to ensure a uniform application across Member States of the selection criteria for Net-Zero Strategic Projects, as laid out in Article 13 of the NZIA.16 These guidelines provide more detailed interpretations of concepts such as "first-of-a-kind" technology, "best available technology," and "significant manufacturing capacity".16

Net-Zero Strategic Projects are set to benefit significantly from their status, including priority treatment in national administrative processes, accelerated permitting timelines, and dedicated financial advice.8 The Commission is encouraging Member States to utilize its centralized online application portal to streamline the process and reduce the administrative load on project promoters.16 While these benefits are substantial, the selection process itself, even with harmonized guidelines, could become a bottleneck if not managed with utmost transparency and efficiency at the national level. The interpretation of criteria like "significant manufacturing capacity" can still be subjective, and the provision allowing a Member State to refuse strategic status if a technology is not aligned with its national energy mix introduces a further layer of potential complexity and divergence.10

**B. The Net-Zero Europe Platform: Structure, sub-groups (strategic projects and financing, access to markets, permitting & valleys, academies), and its role in overseeing NZIA implementation and coordinating financing advice.**

The Net-Zero Europe Platform, formally established by the NZIA and launched on February 19, 2025, serves as the central governance body for the Act's implementation.8 It comprises representatives from all EU Member States and is chaired by the European Commission.8 Its overarching mandate is to oversee the multifaceted implementation of the NZIA, monitor progress towards its objectives, facilitate discussions on proposed actions, engage with civil society and industry stakeholders, and critically, advise on financing for Net-Zero Strategic Projects and foster international net-zero industrial partnerships.1

At its inaugural meeting, four specialized sub-groups were established to manage distinct workstreams, reflecting the NZIA's key pillars 33:

1. **Strategic projects and financing:** This sub-group is tasked with identifying financial bottlenecks and needs at the EU level, coordinating various EU and national funding streams, liaising with the net-zero industry advisory group, and formulating recommendations on financial matters.34
2. **Access to markets:** This sub-group will focus on measures to enhance market access for EU-made net-zero technologies, likely including the practical application of non-price criteria in procurement and auctions.
3. **Permitting and net-zero acceleration valleys:** This sub-group will deal with the challenges of streamlining permitting procedures and coordinating the development and best practices for Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys.
4. **Net-zero academies:** This sub-group will oversee the establishment and functioning of the Net-Zero Industry Academies, aimed at addressing the skills gap in the clean tech sector.

The Platform's success will be a determining factor in ensuring a cohesive and reasonably harmonized application of the NZIA across the diverse regulatory and industrial landscapes of the 27 Member States. Its capacity to foster constructive dialogue, share best practices (for instance, in optimizing permitting processes or designing effective auction mechanisms), and mediate potential disagreements will be thoroughly tested.

Regarding its financial role, the Platform is positioned to act as a crucial coordinator and advisor rather than a direct funding body.8 It is expected to guide strategic projects towards existing EU financial instruments like InvestEU, the Innovation Fund, and the Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP), as well as national resources such as Important Projects of Common European Interest (IPCEIs).32 While this advisory capacity is important for navigating the complex EU funding landscape, the Platform's actual leverage to unlock or direct new financial flows, beyond these existing channels, remains somewhat undefined. Its influence may ultimately depend more on its ability to build consensus, provide compelling strategic guidance, and persuade national and EU-level funding entities than on any direct financial authority it wields. This is particularly relevant given the ongoing debates about the adequacy of overall funding for the NZIA's ambitions.

**C. Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys: Designation criteria, environmental assessment frameworks, public participation mechanisms, and governance.**

The Net-Zero Industry Act empowers Member States to designate specific geographical areas as "Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys" (often referred to as "Valleys").11 These Valleys are envisioned as dedicated zones to foster clusters of net-zero industrial activities, accelerate the manufacturing of Net-Zero Technologies (NZTs), including strategic projects, and provide a testing ground for innovative clean technologies.6 The core idea is to further streamline administrative procedures within these zones and enhance their attractiveness for investment through targeted measures such as infrastructure development, support for private capital, and local workforce reskilling initiatives.11 An early concept for these Valleys also emphasized stimulating industrial symbiosis and concentrating industrial activity to minimize overall environmental impact.36

A key benefit for projects located within these Valleys is the potential for an even faster permitting process, partly due to the possibility of relying on strategic environmental assessments (SEAs) conducted in advance for the Valley as a whole, covering clearly defined industrial sectors.11 This upfront assessment aims to simplify and expedite the environmental approval for individual projects within the designated area. Furthermore, Valleys may benefit from a dedicated single point of contact to assist project promoters.11

Despite these provisions, detailed EU-level guidance on the precise designation criteria for these Valleys, the specific requirements for their overarching environmental plans (beyond individual project EIAs), and the stipulated public consultation mechanisms for the *designation and planning of the Valleys themselves* appears to be limited in the available documentation.8 While individual projects within the Valleys will still be subject to relevant permitting processes, including environmental impact assessments where required by national or EU law (as seen in the German system which integrates EIAs into its immission control permits 11), the framework governing the Valley's own establishment and strategic environmental planning is less clearly defined at the EU level.

This presents a dual scenario: on one hand, the Valleys offer significant potential for accelerating the deployment of net-zero industries by concentrating resources and simplifying processes. On the other hand, the lack of robust, harmonized EU guidance on their designation, comprehensive environmental safeguards for the Valley ecosystem, and transparent public participation in their establishment could lead to concerns. There is a risk that, without such safeguards, these Valleys might be perceived locally as areas where environmental considerations are deprioritized for the sake of speed, potentially leading to local opposition and echoing broader criticisms about accelerated procedures potentially harming communities and the environment.4

The autonomy granted to Member States in designating Valleys and formulating their development plans respects the principle of subsidiarity.11 However, this autonomy could also result in a fragmented landscape of Valleys with varying standards across the EU. This might lead to competitive distortions if some Valleys offer significantly more lenient environmental or social conditions to attract investment. The Net-Zero Europe Platform's sub-group dedicated to "permitting and net-zero acceleration valleys" 33 will likely play a crucial role in promoting coherence, sharing best practices, and potentially developing common minimum criteria or expectations for these zones to ensure they contribute effectively and sustainably to the NZIA's goals.

**III. Funding the Transition: Mechanisms and Debates**

A critical determinant of the Net-Zero Industry Act's success will be the availability and accessibility of adequate financing to support the envisaged scale-up of clean technology manufacturing and deployment. The EU's strategy, as reflected in the NZIA and associated initiatives, largely relies on leveraging existing financial instruments, a point that has sparked considerable debate among stakeholders.

**A. Reliance on Existing EU Funds: Role of the Innovation Fund, InvestEU, REPowerEU, and the Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP).**

The NZIA framework aims to attract substantial investments into the net-zero technology sector.1 However, a prominent and recurring criticism is the apparent absence of significant *new, additional, dedicated EU funding* specifically earmarked for the Act's implementation.10 Instead, the EU's approach centers on mobilizing and redirecting resources from a suite of existing funds and platforms:

- **Innovation Fund:** This is positioned as a key financial instrument supporting NZIA objectives.1 Funded by revenues from the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), its budget is estimated at approximately €40 billion for the 2020-2030 period (based on a carbon price of €75/tCO2).39 The Innovation Fund has launched targeted calls for proposals, such as those in 2024 for net-zero technologies (which sought €21.7 billion against an available €2.4 billion budget) and for electric vehicle battery manufacturing (seeking €1.6 billion for a €1 billion budget), underscoring the high demand for such funding.40 Grants from the Innovation Fund can be combined with other public and private funding sources.39
- **InvestEU:** The InvestEU programme works in synergy with the Innovation Fund, particularly through blending operations.39 Projects recognized as Net-Zero Strategic Projects under the NZIA can receive financing advice that directs them towards InvestEU, among other instruments.32 The Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP) also leverages InvestEU, with proposals to reinforce it by €3 billion, aiming to generate €75 billion in investments.41
- **REPowerEU:** In response to the energy crisis and the need to accelerate the green transition, the European Investment Bank (EIB) significantly increased its commitment under the REPowerEU+ initiative to €45 billion for the period up to 2027.42 This funding explicitly supports the goals of the REPowerEU plan, including the manufacturing of net-zero technologies and the processing of critical raw materials, aligning directly with NZIA objectives.42
- **Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP):** STEP represents a significant effort to pool and strategically direct funding from across 11 existing EU programmes. Through reprioritization and targeted top-ups (e.g., to InvestEU, Innovation Fund, Horizon Europe), STEP aims to mobilize up to €160 billion in new investments in critical sectors including clean technologies, deep tech, and biotech.41 Net-Zero Strategic Projects are specifically mentioned as beneficiaries of financing advice channeled through STEP.32 STEP also introduces a "Sovereignty Seal" to label high-quality projects contributing to its objectives.41

Additionally, the European Hydrogen Bank, which is supported by the Innovation Fund, is designed to unlock private investment in hydrogen value chains by bridging initial investment gaps.1 National resources, such as funding for Important Projects of Common European Interest (IPCEIs), also continue to play a role in supporting large-scale innovative projects.32

This strategy of leveraging and redirecting existing funds demonstrates a pragmatic approach to resource allocation within the EU's budgetary constraints. However, it also raises pertinent questions about the sufficiency of these funds, many of which are already committed to a wide range of EU priorities. The intense competition for funding, as evidenced by the oversubscription of Innovation Fund calls 40, suggests that the available capital may not be adequate to meet the vast investment requirements of the NZIA without potentially diluting the original objectives of these existing instruments.

Furthermore, the reliance on a multitude of distinct funding programmes, each with its own eligibility criteria, application procedures, and timelines, creates a complex and potentially fragmented funding landscape. While the Net-Zero Europe Platform is intended to provide guidance 32, navigating this complexity could pose a significant challenge for project promoters, particularly Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs), potentially slowing down the very investment the NZIA aims to accelerate. This complexity has led to calls from industry bodies like CLEPA for a more "comprehensive five-to-ten-year funding framework" with greater coherence and predictability.45

**B. The "New Money" Debate: Adequacy of current funding, stakeholder calls for dedicated new EU funds, and the status of initiatives like the European Sovereignty Fund.**

The question of financial resources is central to the debate surrounding the Net-Zero Industry Act. A significant and persistent criticism from a wide array of stakeholders, including industry representatives and policy analysts, is the lack of substantial "new money" or additional dedicated EU funding specifically allocated to support the NZIA's ambitious implementation.10 Many observers argue that without fresh financial commitments, the NZIA risks being perceived as offering little more than regulatory streamlining, which, while helpful, may not be sufficient to drive the required scale of investment in EU-based clean tech manufacturing.10

The European Parliament and the Council have also engaged in discussions regarding the adequacy of the NZIA's funding mechanisms, with the "new money" issue being a point of deliberation.46 The investment needs are indeed substantial. European Commission estimates from March 2023 suggested that around €92 billion in investment would be needed for clean tech manufacturing in Europe between 2023 and 2030, with a public funding component of €16 to €18 billion.2 More recent analysis by the Institute for Climate Economics (I4CE) in February 2024 painted an even starker picture, identifying an annual climate investment deficit in the EU of €406 billion to meet 2030 targets across energy, buildings, and transport sectors.47

The concept of a European Sovereignty Fund, which was initially discussed as a potential vehicle to provide substantial new EU-level financing for strategic industries, appears to have evolved or been largely subsumed within the Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP) initiative.41 STEP, as currently structured, focuses primarily on leveraging and redirecting existing EU funds rather than establishing a large, new, standalone financial instrument with fresh capital injections.41 While STEP introduces a "Sovereignty Seal" and aims to steer investments towards enhancing Europe's independence and resilience, its financial architecture is based on optimizing the use of current budgetary resources. The absence of a prominent, separately funded European Sovereignty Fund in recent NZIA-related legislative documents and communications 17 suggests a political preference among Member States for fiscal prudence at the EU level, prioritizing the efficient use of existing funds over new collective borrowing or increased national contributions for such a fund.

This reliance on existing financial frameworks creates a potential structural mismatch between the NZIA's ambitious manufacturing targets (such as producing 40% of annual deployment needs **IV. Economic, Industrial, and Trade Implications**

The Net-Zero Industry Act is poised to have far-reaching implications for the European Union's economy, its industrial landscape, and its international trade relationships. Its success will be measured not only by its contribution to climate goals but also by its ability to foster sustainable economic growth, create quality jobs, enhance industrial competitiveness, and navigate the complex terrain of global trade rules.

**A. Economic Impact Assessment: Projections and analyses on job creation, investment stimulation, and GDP growth.**

The NZIA is fundamentally designed to attract investments and stimulate the creation of quality jobs within the EU's burgeoning clean technology sector.1 The global market for key net-zero technologies is substantial, projected to reach approximately €600 billion annually by 2030, with the EU's own net-zero ecosystem valued at €100 billion in 2021.1 However, realizing the EU's 2030 climate targets necessitates a significant ramp-up in investment. A February 2024 report by I4CE indicated that while EU climate investments reached €407 billion (2.6% of GDP) in 2022, an annual investment of €813 billion (5.1% of GDP) is needed, leaving a yearly deficit of €406 billion.47

While specific, official economic impact assessments from DG GROW detailing precise job, investment, and GDP growth projections directly attributable to the NZIA or its May 2025 secondary legislation were not extensively detailed in the provided materials 1, broader analyses of the green transition offer valuable context. Deloitte's modeling, for instance, suggests that an active global transition to net-zero could generate over 300 million additional jobs worldwide by 2050 compared to a scenario of inaction.50 Crucially, this modeling also indicates that approximately 80% of the skills required for this transition already exist within the current workforce, highlighting the importance of reskilling and upskilling initiatives like the Net-Zero Industry Academies.50

The World Economic Forum's 2024 Net Zero Industry Tracker estimates that an additional $30 trillion in capital investment will be required globally by 2050 for eight hard-to-abate industrial sectors, with 43% of this investment needing to come from the sectors themselves and 57% for associated clean energy infrastructure.51 Analyses by E3G suggest that decisive investment in the green transition could yield a net positive impact on GDP of 2.8%, potentially rising to 4.7% when the benefits of avoided environmental damage are factored in.52 Similarly, Agora Energiewende has concluded that the clean energy transition is likely to boost GDP and employment, with the co-benefits, such as avoided health costs, outweighing the additional investment expenditures.53

The European Commission's Spring 2025 Economic Forecast, while not specific to the NZIA, projects overall EU GDP growth at 1.1% in 2025 and 1.5% in 2026. Investment is forecast to grow by 1.5% in 2025 and 2.4% in 2026, with an anticipated addition of 2 million jobs over these two years and a decline in the unemployment rate to a historic low of 5.7% by 2026.49 These general economic trends provide a backdrop against which the NZIA's impacts will unfold.

The sheer scale of the investment needed—as highlighted by I4CE's €406 billion annual deficit 47—far exceeds the direct public funding capacity of the EU and its Member States. Consequently, the NZIA's success in stimulating economic activity will heavily depend on its ability to effectively de-risk projects and create compelling market conditions that attract substantial private capital. In this context, the provisions for "increasing planning and investment certainty" 8 and "cutting red tape" 1 become critical levers. If these regulatory streamlining efforts fail to meaningfully reduce barriers and perceived risks for investors, the significant investment gap is likely to persist, hampering the Act's economic ambitions.

Furthermore, while positive net job creation is anticipated from the green transition, the NZIA's implementation must also address the significant job *transformation* that will occur. As traditional industries decarbonize and new clean tech sectors expand, there will be job displacement in some areas and a demand for new skill sets in others. The NZIA's emphasis on Net-Zero Industry Academies 1 acknowledges this challenge, but achieving the Act's aspiration for "quality jobs" will necessitate proactive and comprehensive workforce development policies at both EU and national levels to manage this transition effectively and equitably.

**B. Enhancing EU Competitiveness and Resilience: Addressing supply chain vulnerabilities (particularly concerning China in solar PV and batteries) and the role of resilience criteria.**

A primary driver behind the Net-Zero Industry Act is the imperative to enhance the European Union's industrial competitiveness and bolster the resilience of its clean technology supply chains.8 The EU's significant reliance on external suppliers for key net-zero technologies has become a strategic concern. This dependency is particularly pronounced with China, which dominates global manufacturing in sectors like solar photovoltaics (PV) – where EU dependence on Chinese ingots and wafers exceeds 90% – and batteries.5 Compounding this, Chinese-made batteries are reportedly 32% less expensive and solar panels 65% less expensive than their European-produced counterparts, posing a significant cost challenge for EU manufacturers.48

The NZIA seeks to address these vulnerabilities through several mechanisms, with resilience criteria embedded in public procurement and renewable energy auctions playing a central role.8 These criteria are designed to be triggered when there is an "excessive dependency" on a single third-country supplier for a specific technology or its main components.16 The May 2025 secondary legislation package included a crucial Communication from the Commission providing data on the origins of the EU's net-zero technology supply, thereby informing the application of these resilience criteria and assisting Member States in evaluating projects for strategic status.16

The introduction of such resilience criteria will likely necessitate the acceptance of a "resilience premium" – potentially higher initial costs for some net-zero technologies – to facilitate diversification away from dominant, lower-cost overseas suppliers. The NZIA itself acknowledges this by allowing non-price criteria to be bypassed if they lead to disproportionate cost increases (e.g., over 15%, as cited by Eurofer in the context of wind auctions 30). The political and societal willingness to bear this premium in exchange for enhanced long-term supply security and industrial sovereignty will be a critical factor in the successful implementation of these measures.

Beyond merely shifting sourcing patterns, well-designed resilience criteria could also act as a catalyst for innovation within EU manufacturing. By creating a more predictable market for resilient, domestically produced technologies, these criteria can incentivize European companies to invest in next-generation manufacturing processes aimed at closing cost gaps with international competitors and enhancing technological leadership. This aligns with the broader goals of the EU's Clean Industrial Deal, which frames energy security and emissions reduction as twin objectives and encourages reshoring and "Buy European" approaches in procurement.56 Recent EU trade patterns (2021-2023) already indicate a nascent shift in imports away from countries without trade agreements towards intra-EU sources, regional neighbors, and partners with active trade initiatives, reflecting a broader trend towards curbing dependencies.57

**C. WTO Compatibility: Analysis of NZIA provisions, particularly resilience and potential local content aspects, in the context of international trade law.**

The Net-Zero Industry Act's provisions, particularly those related to resilience criteria and the implicit goal of boosting domestic manufacturing, operate within the complex framework of international trade law, primarily governed by the World Trade Organization (WTO). The increasing adoption of Green Industrial Policies (GIPs) globally, which often blend climate objectives with economic and geostrategic aims, inherently raises questions about their compatibility with WTO rules.58 Ensuring that such policies are "WTO-proof" is crucial for maintaining a rules-based multilateral trading system and avoiding the escalation of trade tensions.58

A key concern revolves around whether the NZIA's resilience criteria could be interpreted as de facto local content requirements (LCRs). LCRs, which typically mandate the use of a certain proportion of domestically produced goods or services, are generally considered inconsistent with WTO principles, notably the national treatment obligation under Article III of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT).55 The EU has historically championed a rules-based trading order, making adherence to WTO commitments a significant consideration.55

The Centre for European Policy (CEP) study on resilience auctions specifically advocates for designing these criteria as "diversification criteria" rather than explicit LCRs.55 A diversification criterion, aimed at reducing over-reliance on *any* single dominant supplier (regardless of its origin, including potentially an EU-based one if it became overly dominant), rather than explicitly favoring EU content or targeting specific countries, would likely be more defensible under WTO law. The NZIA's framing of "excessive dependency on a single source of supply" 16 appears to align with this diversification logic.

However, the practical application of these criteria will be critical. If the resilience measures are implemented in a manner that disproportionately impacts certain trading partners without clear, objective, and non-discriminatory justification beyond diversification, they could still face challenges at the WTO. The global context of rising GIPs and associated trade frictions makes this a particularly sensitive area.58

Even if the EU meticulously designs its resilience criteria to be WTO-compliant, the substantial economic interests at stake in the burgeoning global market for net-zero technologies mean that affected trading partners might nevertheless initiate WTO disputes. Such litigation could create legal uncertainty and potentially deter investment, irrespective of the final outcome. The current paralysis of the WTO's Appellate Body further complicates the dispute settlement landscape, potentially leading to prolonged disputes without definitive resolution.59 The EU is also concurrently deploying other trade-related instruments, such as the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) 60, and has expressed concerns about unfair international competition and subsidies provided by third countries 62, adding layers to an already intricate trade policy environment. The NZIA must therefore navigate a fine line between legitimately fostering domestic industrial capacity and supply chain resilience, and avoiding measures that could be construed as discriminatory protectionism.

**V. Challenges, Criticisms, and Stakeholder Perspectives**

The Net-Zero Industry Act, while ambitious in its scope and objectives, has elicited a range of reactions from stakeholders and faces several significant challenges that could impact its successful implementation and long-term efficacy. These concerns span funding adequacy, the breadth of its technological focus, administrative complexities, and potential environmental and social trade-offs.

**A. Key Concerns: Funding shortfalls, scope limitations (too broad or too narrow), administrative burdens, and potential negative environmental or social impacts.**

A primary and widely voiced concern is the **perceived shortfall in dedicated new EU funding** for the NZIA.10 Many stakeholders argue that without substantial fresh financial commitments, the Act's impact may be limited primarily to regulatory streamlining, which, while beneficial, might not be sufficient to catalyze the massive investments required to meet its targets.10

The **scope of the NZIA** has also drawn criticism from multiple angles. Some argue that by targeting a broad array of 19-plus technologies, the Act lacks strategic focus and risks diluting resources that could be more effectively concentrated on a smaller set of proven, high-impact solutions.10 Environmental NGOs, in particular, have expressed apprehension that supporting expensive and potentially unproven technologies, such as certain forms of nuclear energy or large-scale carbon capture and storage (CCS) without strict limitations, could divert vital public funds from readily deployable renewable energy solutions and energy efficiency measures.4

Regarding **administrative burdens and the push for accelerated permitting**, while the NZIA aims to reduce red tape, there are fears that these expedited processes could inadvertently lead to negative consequences. Critics worry about increased pressure on local communities and the environment, the potential overburdening of national and regional public authorities tasked with implementing these new procedures, and an exacerbation of conflicts at the local level if due diligence and public participation are compromised.4 NGOs have emphasized that sustainable industrial development requires more than just fast-tracked permits; it necessitates robust environmental impact assessments, meaningful stakeholder engagement, and adequate resources for permitting authorities.4 The German Association of Energy and Water Industries (BDEW) also cautioned against the creation of new bureaucratic hurdles in one area while attempting to simplify others.64

There are also apprehensions about the **limited practical application** of some NZIA provisions, particularly those concerning public procurement and auctions, if not robustly designed and enforced.10

Finally, **potential negative environmental and social impacts** are a significant concern for civil society organizations. They point to the risks of deregulation affecting public participation rights and biodiversity, with specific calls to exclude industrial developments like Net-Zero Industry Valleys from ecologically sensitive areas such as Natura 2000 sites.4 Broader anxieties have been fueled by initiatives like the Omnibus Simplification Package, which some NGOs believe weakens corporate sustainability due diligence and environmental and human rights protections by bypassing proper impact assessments and broad public consultation.65 This creates a tension between the NZIA's goal of rapid industrial scale-up and the imperative to uphold high environmental and social standards, a "speed vs. quality" dilemma that requires careful navigation.

**B. Industry Reactions: Perspectives from key associations (e.g., WindEurope, EASE, CEFIC, BusinessEurope, Eurofer, SolarPower Europe) on the NZIA and its implementing acts.**

European industry associations have generally welcomed the overarching goals of the Net-Zero Industry Act – to bolster domestic manufacturing, enhance competitiveness, and streamline regulatory processes. However, their support is often qualified, accompanied by specific demands for stronger measures, greater clarity, and a more conducive investment environment.

**SolarPower Europe** expressed urgency following the May 2025 secondary legislation, emphasizing that "solar manufacturers in peril, there is no time to lose" and calling upon Member States to effectively implement resilience principles, including potential premiums for solar panels made in Europe.19 This highlights the acute competitive pressures faced by the EU solar manufacturing sector.

**WindEurope**, in its February 2025 response to consultations on implementing acts, stressed the critical need for clear, harmonized, and implementable rules for non-price criteria in auctions to avoid a patchwork of "27 different sets of rules" across Member States.31 In April 2025, the association proposed a "New Offshore Wind Pact," advocating for auctions of at least 100GW of new offshore wind capacity between 2031 and 2040, utilizing Contracts for Difference (CfDs), and committing to a 30% reduction in the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) by 2040 in return.67 While welcoming the Clean Industrial Deal State Aid Framework (CISAF) as a continuation of the effective Temporary Crisis and Transition Framework (TCTF), WindEurope also noted that some of its provisions were too restrictive.66

**Eurofer**, representing the steel industry and contributing to a joint wind supply chain position paper in December 2024, adopted a more assertive stance. It called for non-price criteria to apply to 100% of auction volumes (not just the mandated 30%), recommended limiting non-EU sourcing to 25% per GPA country for components, and emphasized the need for consistency with the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM).30 Eurofer also argued for a comprehensive list of "Primarily Used" and "Main Specific Components" for wind technology, including towers and foundations, and advocated for compulsory resilience criteria ensuring at least 60% EU sourcing for these key components, assessed at the individual component level rather than aggregated financial value.30 This position reflected a disagreement with WindEurope's perceived preference for a more limited list of main components, underscoring differing priorities within the broader clean tech value chain.30

The **European Association for Storage of Energy (EASE)**, in its earlier analyses (March 2024), acknowledged the NZIA's positive aims of increasing EU manufacturing capacity and streamlining permitting, noting the inclusion of batteries and energy storage as Net-Zero Strategic Technologies.2 While specific reactions from EASE to the May 2025 implementing acts were not prominent in the provided materials, the broader energy storage sector stands to benefit from the NZIA's focus.

The **European Chemical Industry Council (CEFIC)** has primarily focused on related regulatory areas like the simplification of REACH and the development of sustainable carbon cycles.68 Their positions underscore the chemical industry's need for a predictable regulatory environment and competitive access to alternative feedstocks to support its decarbonization, which indirectly relates to the NZIA's success in fostering a broader clean industrial ecosystem.

**BusinessEurope**, in a May 2025 paper on water policy, highlighted that the success of initiatives like the NZIA, the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA), and the Chips Act is contingent upon the availability of water resources, as many net-zero technologies are water-intensive in their manufacturing processes.70 General concerns from business associations often revolve around the overall competitiveness of the EU and the cumulative regulatory burden.61 The fact that nearly 50 new components were added to the NZIA's strategic list following consultations that included business associations indicates a degree of responsiveness from the Commission to industry input.17

The **German Association of Energy and Water Industries (BDEW)** expressed support for the NZIA's objectives but voiced concerns about the potential for new bureaucratic burdens, offering specific comments on the implementing acts related to non-price criteria such as responsible business conduct and cybersecurity.64

Overall, while industry sectors are keen to leverage the opportunities presented by the NZIA, there is a clear call for more robust market-pull mechanisms, greater regulatory clarity and harmonization, and tangible support to ensure a level playing field against international competitors. The varied perspectives on specific details, such as the scope of component lists or the stringency of sourcing criteria, reflect the diverse interests and challenges across different segments of the net-zero technology value chain.

**C. NGO and Civil Society Views: Concerns regarding environmental safeguards, public participation, and the focus of the Act (e.g., EEB, CAN Europe, ClientEarth).**

Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and civil society groups have approached the Net-Zero Industry Act with significant caution, primarily raising concerns about the potential erosion of environmental safeguards, the adequacy of public participation mechanisms, and the Act's overall strategic focus.

A coalition including **WWF, CAN Europe, Transport & Environment (T&E), and the European Environmental Bureau (EEB)**, in statements preceding the NZIA's finalization (November 2023), expressed apprehension regarding the definition of strategic net-zero technologies.4 They warned against potential negative impacts stemming from deregulation on public participation, local communities, and biodiversity. A key point of contention was the potential diversion of public funds towards expensive and unproven technologies, such as nuclear fusion or Small Modular Reactors (SMRs), at the expense of readily available and cost-effective solutions like wind, solar, and energy efficiency.4 They also argued for a very limited and targeted use of Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS), restricted to genuinely unavoidable industrial emissions, and called for the explicit exclusion of Net-Zero Industry Valleys from ecologically sensitive Natura 2000 sites, emphasizing the need for proper spatial planning and thorough environmental impact assessments (EIAs).4

The **EEB**, reacting to the final NZIA deal (likely February 2024), reiterated these criticisms, stating that the Act "gambles on expensive and unproven solutions" and that the broad inclusion of technologies like nuclear energy and CCS (without strict limitations) would stretch scarce public funds and minimize impact.21 They argued that the NZIA's simplistic approach, based on shorter permitting procedures and reduced public participation, would likely result in additional pressure on EU communities and the environment, potentially overburdening national authorities and exacerbating local conflicts.21

**ClientEarth** has focused on broader concerns about the potential weakening of corporate accountability and environmental and human rights protections under the EU's simplification agenda. In February and May 2025, they criticized the "Omnibus Simplification Package" – which seeks to amend key corporate sustainability laws like the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) – for allegedly diminishing due diligence obligations and diluting climate transition plan requirements.65 ClientEarth welcomed an inquiry by the EU Ombudsman into the European Commission's preparation of this package, citing a lack of proper impact assessment and broad public consultation.65 These concerns, while not solely about the NZIA, reflect a deep-seated skepticism among some NGOs that the drive for industrial competitiveness and regulatory streamlining might come at the cost of hard-won environmental and social safeguards.

A joint NGO statement, issued prior to the NZIA's finalization, underscored that only "truly clean technologies" should receive support and that effective green industrial development requires more than just fast-track permitting; it demands that public authorities also fulfill their role in safeguarding governance, democracy, biodiversity, and human health, with meaningful community and public involvement.63

These perspectives highlight a fundamental distrust among many NGOs regarding the "streamlining" agenda if it is not accompanied by equally robust, or even strengthened, environmental and participatory safeguards. They perceive an inherent tension where the pursuit of speed and industrial scale-up under the NZIA could override due process and environmental protection. The inclusion and promotion of technologies like CCS and nuclear within the NZIA framework remains a particularly divisive issue, with environmental groups viewing it as a misallocation of resources that could perpetuate fossil fuel dependencies or create new long-term environmental liabilities, rather than focusing squarely on accelerating the deployment of renewable energy and efficiency measures.4

**VI. National Implementation Strategies and Challenges**

The effective rollout of the Net-Zero Industry Act is heavily contingent on the actions and capacities of individual Member States. While the NZIA provides an EU-level framework, its translation into concrete outcomes will vary based on national priorities, industrial structures, administrative capabilities, and political will.

**A. Overview of Member State Approaches: General challenges in transposing and implementing NZIA provisions.**

Member States are tasked with several key responsibilities under the NZIA. These include designating national competent authorities to facilitate and coordinate permit-granting processes, providing administrative support to project promoters (with a particular focus on SMEs), and ensuring that relevant information on permitting, financing, and support services is easily accessible.10 From 2026, they will need to implement the new rules for applying non-price criteria in at least 30% of their renewable energy auctions.19 Furthermore, Member States have the authority to designate Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys and are required to develop strategic plans for these areas to enhance their attractiveness for net-zero industrial activities.11 They must also report annually on CO2 capture projects.35

However, the implementation journey is fraught with challenges. A significant hurdle identified in the broader context of European Green Deal implementation is the issue of **administrative capacity constraints** at the national and regional levels, often stemming from a high policy load and the rapid pace of legislative change.71 The NZIA adds another layer of complex tasks, including establishing new permitting workflows, designing sophisticated auction mechanisms, and overseeing the development of industrial valleys. This could overburden public authorities, particularly in Member States with more limited resources.10

Another challenge is ensuring **policy coherence**, both vertically (between EU and national levels) and horizontally (across different national policy domains).71 The NZIA allows Member States a degree of flexibility, for instance, in deciding the extent to which they apply non-price criteria beyond the 30% minimum, or even in refusing to grant "strategic project" status to a project if the technology it promotes is not accepted as part of their national energy mix.10 While this respects national prerogatives, it also creates a significant risk of **implementation heterogeneity** across the EU. Such divergence could lead to an unlevel playing field for businesses, fragment the single market for net-zero technologies, and ultimately hinder the NZIA's overall effectiveness. This resonates with concerns voiced by industry bodies like WindEurope about the potential emergence of "27 different sets of rules".31

The development of a **skilled workforce** also remains a critical cross-cutting challenge for the successful manufacturing and deployment of net-zero technologies.10 While the NZIA envisages Net-Zero Industry Academies, national efforts in education, training, and reskilling will be paramount. The role of regional authorities, and bodies like the European Committee of the Regions, will be important in ensuring that the transition is just and that no region is left behind, although specific opinions from the Committee on NZIA implementation were not detailed in the provided materials.60

**B. Case Studies (Selected Member States e.g., France, Germany, Spain, Poland, Italy): Specific national plans, support schemes, and identified challenges.**

The NZIA's implementation is taking shape differently across Member States, reflecting their unique industrial landscapes, energy strategies, and regulatory traditions.

- **Germany:** Is leveraging its existing robust administrative structures. Single points of contact for NZIA permitting are being established at the federal state level.11 Germany's established immission control permit procedures, which can be faster than NZIA timelines and already integrate Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs) and public participation for formal processes, offer a pathway for NZT projects.11 The "Joint Task for the Improvement of Regional Economic Structures" (GRW) program, a long-standing regional development tool, has been expanded to specifically support investments in net-zero technologies in designated regions.43
- **France:** Aims for 18 GW of installed offshore wind capacity by 2035 but faces significant technical, political, and industrial hurdles.73 While advocating for a strong and intelligent implementation of the NZIA, France is concerned that the Act's resilience criteria, if not carefully calibrated, could lead to higher price caps for offshore wind projects. The nation possesses a solid base of Tier 2 to Tier 5 suppliers but faces challenges with Tier 1 companies and turbine manufacturers, where European demand currently outstrips supply.73 France is also collaborating with Italy and Greece on developing CCS infrastructure.74
- **Spain:** Is actively promoting the development of cleantech industrial clusters and views its new national Industry Act as a key enabler.75 Spain's strategy emphasizes creating clear demand signals for low-carbon products, fostering robust public-private partnerships, simplifying permitting processes (including through "one-stop-shops"), deploying innovative financing mechanisms (like blended finance, green bonds, and Contracts for Difference), and leveraging ETS revenues for cleantech manufacturing.75 The country has been a major recipient of foreign direct investment in renewable energy projects and has substantial funding allocated from the EU's Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) for its green transition.76
- **Poland:** Faces a particularly complex transition due to its heavy reliance on coal, which constitutes around 70% of its power generation.77 The decarbonization process will have significant regional economic impacts, especially in coal-dependent areas. Poland's updated National Energy and Climate Plan (NECP) is undergoing consultation, with key challenges including the deployment of a diverse portfolio of low- and zero-carbon technologies, developing robust energy system modeling, ensuring targeted (rather than dispersed) use of clean hydrogen (e.g., for hard-to-abate industries), and critically, managing a just transition for its coal regions and workforce.78
- **Italy:** Has a long-term strategy for achieving carbon neutrality by 2050, which includes significant roles for both CCS and Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR).74 Several CCS projects are under development, with some having achieved Project of Common Interest (PCI) status. Italy is partnering with France and Greece on CCS infrastructure development and is utilizing RRF funds to support Rome's climate neutrality goals and broader green transition initiatives, including biomethane development.74 Its Climate Change and Energy Transition Law of 2021 provides the overarching national framework.44
- **Netherlands:** While specific NZIA implementation plans were not detailed, general concerns from Dutch-based industry (via CLEPA) point to the need for NZIA to be part of a holistic EU industrial strategy that ensures predictability, matches international competition, and addresses the shortcomings of current funding opportunities, which are seen as lacking coherence and having unclear eligibility criteria.45

These national approaches demonstrate that Member States are interpreting and operationalizing the NZIA through the prism of their existing industrial strengths, energy policy priorities, and established regulatory cultures. For instance, Germany's reliance on its well-developed immission control system, Spain's focus on its renewable energy potential and industrial clusters, France's strategic attention to offshore wind, and Poland's overriding concern with the coal transition all shape their respective NZIA pathways. This inherent diversity underscores the challenge for the EU in achieving a harmonized and uniformly effective implementation of the Act.

Furthermore, the "just transition" imperative is particularly acute for countries like Poland. While the NZIA's provisions for Net-Zero Industry Academies and the potential for Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys to be located in less developed or Just Transition Fund regions offer some support 32, the scale of socio-economic restructuring required in coal-reliant regions may necessitate more substantial and targeted national and EU-level Just Transition funding, integrated closely with NZIA initiatives.

**VII. Conclusion and Strategic Outlook**

The Net-Zero Industry Act represents a landmark legislative effort by the European Union, signaling a determined push to align its ambitious climate neutrality goals with a robust industrial strategy. It aims to transform the EU into a leading manufacturing hub for the clean technologies that will power the future, thereby enhancing its economic competitiveness, bolstering its strategic autonomy, and accelerating the green transition.

**A. Summary of NZIA's potential to reshape EU industry.**

The NZIA holds considerable potential to reshape the EU's industrial fabric. By setting clear manufacturing benchmarks, streamlining permitting processes, and fostering demand for domestically produced net-zero technologies through reformed public procurement and auction mechanisms, the Act seeks to create a more predictable and attractive investment environment. Provisions for Net-Zero Strategic Projects, Acceleration Valleys, Industry Academies, and regulatory sandboxes are designed to catalyze innovation, cluster industrial activity, develop a skilled workforce, and reduce time-to-market for new technologies. If effectively implemented, the NZIA could stimulate significant private investment, drive the creation of high-quality green jobs, secure critical supply chains against external disruptions, and position European industries at the forefront of the rapidly expanding global market for clean technologies. This, in turn, would contribute to the EU's energy independence and its ability to meet its 2030 and 2050 climate targets.

**B. Key outstanding challenges and opportunities for successful implementation.**

Despite its potential, the NZIA faces a multitude of significant challenges. Securing adequate, sustained, and easily accessible **funding** remains paramount, with ongoing debate about the sufficiency of leveraging existing EU funds versus the need for substantial new financial commitments. Ensuring consistent, effective, and harmonized **Member State implementation** is another critical hurdle, given national specificities and potential capacity constraints. The Act must navigate the inherent tension between the demand for **rapid deployment and the imperative to uphold robust environmental and social safeguards**, including meaningful public participation. The design and application of **resilience criteria** in a manner that is both effective in diversifying supply chains and compliant with international trade law (WTO) will require careful balancing. Furthermore, managing the **skills transition** by adequately preparing the workforce for the evolving demands of net-zero industries is crucial for a just and successful transformation.

Conversely, significant opportunities exist. The NZIA can leverage the strength of the **EU single market** to create scale and demand for European clean technologies. Fostering **cross-border collaboration** through initiatives like Net-Zero Acceleration Valleys or enhanced Important Projects of Common European Interest (IPCEIs) can pool resources and expertise. The strategic use of **public procurement and auction design**, incorporating well-defined non-price criteria, can act as a powerful market-pull mechanism. Capitalizing on the EU's existing strengths in **research and innovation** can help develop next-generation technologies and maintain a competitive edge.

**C. Concluding expert assessment on the NZIA's long-term viability and impact.**

The Net-Zero Industry Act is a necessary and strategically vital initiative for the European Union. In an era of intensifying global competition for clean technology leadership and heightened geopolitical considerations around supply chain security, doing nothing was not a viable option. The Act provides a much-needed framework to steer industrial development in line with the EU's climate ambitions.

However, its success is far from guaranteed and will depend critically on several factors. Sustained **political will** at both EU and national levels is essential to maintain momentum and overcome inevitable obstacles. **Adequate resourcing**, from both public and private sectors, must be mobilized to match the scale of the ambition; the current reliance on existing funds may prove insufficient without further innovation in financing mechanisms or new capital injections. **Adaptive governance**, particularly through the Net-Zero Europe Platform, will be crucial for coordinating diverse actors, sharing best practices, resolving conflicts, and ensuring that the NZIA framework can evolve in response to technological advancements and changing market dynamics. The provisions for future reviews, such as the CO2 storage target in 2028 27, and the use of delegated acts to update component lists 17, indicate an understanding of this need for adaptability.

The May 2025 secondary legislation provided essential operational clarifications, moving the NZIA from a broad framework to a more actionable set of rules. However, the real test lies in the consistent and effective application and enforcement of these rules across all Member States. A pragmatic approach that acknowledges and balances competing objectives – speed versus due diligence, strategic focus versus technological inclusivity, domestic preference versus international trade obligations – will be indispensable.

Ultimately, the NZIA should not be viewed as a panacea or a static piece of legislation. It is a critical component of a broader, evolving ecosystem of EU policies encompassing climate action, energy, trade, competition, skills, and regional development. Its long-term viability and impact will be determined by its ability to integrate seamlessly with these wider policy agendas, to genuinely empower Member States and industry to innovate and invest, and to adapt to the dynamic global landscape. Continuous monitoring, rigorous evaluation of its impacts, and a collective willingness to refine and strengthen the framework will be essential if the Net-Zero Industry Act is to fulfill its promise of positioning the European Union as a true leader in the global net-zero industrial age.
